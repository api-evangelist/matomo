<p>Data privacy regulations are evolving, customer expectations are rising and businesses need the right tools to build trust and stay compliant.&nbsp;</p>



<p>Data privacy management software comes in many different forms. There are consent managers, mapping tools, breach response systems, vendor risk platforms, and more.&nbsp;</p>



<p>This guide explains the main categories of privacy management software, what each type does and when to use it. We’ll also show you how to map your organisation’s needs to the right type of tool and highlight five tools that showcase different approaches to data privacy management<a href="https://docs.google.com/document/d/11mCYG0tQMy2vu2u5f-i5lhIuiM_H_SqKxp-HxSvlPsA/edit?tab=t.0#heading=h.4jgvl7o03nmu"></a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What is data privacy management software?&nbsp;</h2>



<p>Data privacy management software helps businesses properly handle personal data, protect user privacy and comply with privacy laws such as the GDPR and CCPA, as well as other global regulations. These platforms range from simple consent tracking tools to comprehensive systems for ensuring compliance across an entire organisation.&nbsp;</p>



<p>Here are some of the standard features:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Consent management:&nbsp;</strong>Collecting and recording&nbsp;<a href="https://matomo.org/subcategory/consent/">user consent</a>&nbsp;for data collection and processing activities.&nbsp;</li>



<li><strong>Data subject request handling:&nbsp;</strong>Automating and tracking requests from people who want to access, correct or delete their data.&nbsp;</li>



<li><strong>Granular tracking and auditing</strong>: Monitoring data flows across systems, providing a detailed record of who accessed what and when.&nbsp;</li>



<li><strong>Policy automation and compliance templates</strong>: Simplifying compliance with&nbsp;<a href="https://matomo.org/faq/general/faq_152/">privacy policy</a>&nbsp;templates and automatic updates as regulations change.&nbsp;</li>



<li><strong>Third-party risk management:</strong>&nbsp;Verifying that external tools and partners follow the same privacy and compliance standards.&nbsp;</li>



<li><strong>Customisable reporting and alerts:&nbsp;</strong>Automated reporting and custom notifications to identify compliance risks early.&nbsp;</li>
</ul>



<p>The primary objective of these tools is to enhance data privacy protections and support compliance with requirements such as the ePrivacy Directive implementing laws (e.g., PERC (the UK), TDDDG (Germany), LSSI (Spain), TKG (Austria), the&nbsp;<a href="https://matomo.org/blog/2023/09/gdpr-personal-data/">General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)</a>&nbsp;and the&nbsp;<a href="https://matomo.org/blog/2023/08/ccpa-compliance/">California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA)</a>.</p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Consent management software</h3>



<p><a href="https://matomo.org/blog/2025/02/guide-consent-manager-platform-integrations-cmp/">Consent management software</a>&nbsp;collects, records and manages user consent for data processing. The platforms display cookie consent banners or pop-ups that inform users about how their data will be used. Users can then choose which types of data collection they accept.&nbsp;</p>



<p>The software stores these preferences and updates records if someone changes their settings. For example, if a user wants to withdraw their contact information, the system updates to reflect this change.&nbsp;</p>



<p>It logs every consent action in accordance with relevant privacy laws, such as the&nbsp;<a href="https://matomo.org/faq/general/eprivacy-directive-national-implementations-and-website-analytics/">ePrivacy Directive</a>, which requires opt-in for all trackers and non-essential cookies.</p>



<p>Privacy-centric analytics platforms, like&nbsp;<a href="https://matomo.org/">Matomo</a>, also support&nbsp;<a href="https://matomo.org/faq/matomo-google-consent-mode-v2/">Consent Mode</a>. This means tracking is adjusted based on user choices.</p>



<p><strong>Note:</strong>&nbsp;Consent is one&nbsp;<a href="https://matomo.org/blog/2018/04/lawful-basis-for-processing-personal-data-under-gdpr-with-matomo/">lawful basis under GDPR</a>. Some data processing activities may use other bases, such as contractual requirements or legal obligations.</p>



<p><strong>Best for:&nbsp;</strong>Businesses that collect personal data from users online and need to maintain transparent records for compliance.&nbsp;</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Data mapping and inventory software</h3>



<p>Data mapping and inventory software identify where personal data is stored and how it flows across systems. The platforms automatically scan databases, servers and cloud tools to locate personal information and map its journey within the organisation.&nbsp;</p>



<p>This visibility is crucial for data governance. It helps businesses understand:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>What data they have</li>



<li>Where it resides&nbsp;</li>



<li>How/With whom it’s shared</li>
</ul>



<p>The system monitors who&#8217;s accessing data and why, giving compliance teams a clear picture of data handling practices. This helps them spot potential risks early on.</p>



<p><strong>Best for:&nbsp;</strong>Organisations that need visibility into where personal data is stored and how it&#8217;s used across systems.&nbsp;</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Privacy risk assessment software</h3>



<p>Privacy risk assessment software lets businesses identify and mitigate potential data breaches. The technology assesses how personal data is collected, stored and shared, and assigns risk levels accordingly.&nbsp;</p>



<p>The software also helps businesses conduct&nbsp;<a href="https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/law-enforcement/guide-to-le-processing/accountability-and-governance/data-protection-impact-assessments/">Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIA)</a>, which are a key requirement under GDPR. Other privacy laws globally also require data controllers to carry out privacy impact assessments. The system:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Documents the purpose of data processing</li>



<li>Assesses potential privacy risks</li>



<li>Evaluates the necessity and proportionality of the activity</li>



<li>Records mitigate measures&nbsp;</li>
</ul>



<p><strong>Best for:&nbsp;</strong>Companies performing privacy impact assessments for new data processing activities or third-party technologies.&nbsp;</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Data subject rights management (DSR) software</h3>



<p>DSR software automates Data Subject Access Requests (DSARs), such as when individuals request access to, correct or delete their personal information. The platform speeds up request intake, verifies identities and tracks progress to ensure responses meet legal timeframes.&nbsp;</p>



<p>Each request is logged and managed through a central dashboard, reducing manual effort and helping businesses meet their applicable privacy law or other compliance obligations.</p>



<p><strong>Best for:&nbsp;</strong>Businesses that regularly receive data requests and need to manage them quickly and accurately.&nbsp;</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Breach and incident management software</h3>



<p>Breach and incident software detects, documents and responds to data breaches or security incidents. The platforms automatically log potential breaches, assess their severity and guide teams through the best way to address or mitigate the issue.&nbsp;</p>



<p>Here are some of the common causes of data breaches:&nbsp;</p>



<figure class="wp-block-image size-full"><img alt="Lists lost devices/papers, misdirected email, cyber theft of personal data, and ransomware." class="wp-image-91329" height="586" src="https://matomo.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Common-Data-Breach-Scenarios.png" width="1024" /></figure>



<p></p>



<p>Data breach features allow organisations to respond quickly to these incidents, reducing damage and maintaining compliance.&nbsp;</p>



<p>The software helps teams assess whether the incident requires regulatory reporting and prepares notifications for authorities and affected individuals.&nbsp;</p>



<p><strong>Best for:&nbsp;</strong>Organisations that need a reliable data breach and incident response process.&nbsp;</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Third-party risk management software</h3>



<p>Third-party risk management systems evaluate and monitor the privacy practices of external vendors and partners. This means businesses can identify potential compliance gaps and reduce the risk of data breaches through their vendor networks.&nbsp;</p>



<p>It uses automated questionnaires, risk scoring and continuous monitoring techniques to verify that third parties meet compliance standards.&nbsp;</p>



<p>The platform also stores documentation, such as contracts, certifications, and audit reports to provide an up-to-date record of each vendor’s compliance status. Alerts immediately notify teams of changes or risks, so they can respond quickly.&nbsp;</p>



<p>With&nbsp;<a href="https://matomo.org/faq/tag-manager/using-onetrust-consent-manager-cmp-with-matomo-tag-manager/">Matomo&#8217;s OneTrust Tag Manager integration</a>, teams can align tracking practices with their broader third‑party management processes and vendor risk workflows.&nbsp;</p>



<p><strong>Best for:&nbsp;</strong>Privacy operations that rely on external vendors and need to ensure they comply with data protection laws.&nbsp;</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Data anonymisation, pseudonymisation and tokenisation software</h3>



<p>Data anonymisation software permanently and irreversibly removes or alters identifiers so that they cannot be linked back to an individual, making personal information unidentifiable. If effectively anonymised, datasets fall outside the scope of privacy laws, such as the GDPR.&nbsp;</p>



<p>By removing or replacing identifiers with tokens and prioritising&nbsp;<a href="https://matomo.org/blog/2025/10/data-minimisation/">data minimisation</a>, businesses protect personal information.&nbsp;</p>



<p>Masking, encryption and tokenisation usually create&nbsp;<em>pseudonymised</em>&nbsp;data, which still counts as personal data under GDPR, even though it’s better protected</p>



<p><strong>Best for:&nbsp;</strong>Organisations that analyse large datasets but must protect individuals&#8217; identities and comply with privacy regulations.&nbsp;</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What’s in store for data privacy in 2026?&nbsp;</h2>



<p>Data privacy is evolving rapidly, driven by stricter regulations, growing consumer expectations and the rise of AI.&nbsp;</p>



<p>More countries are implementing privacy and AI laws, making global compliance far more complex. Here are a couple of examples:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>New EU and UK developments</strong></li>
</ul>



<p>Evolving privacy obligations in 2026 include the EU’s Digital Omnibus Act and the UK’s updated Privacy and Electronic Regulation Code (PERC). These frameworks are strengthening cookie consent rules, cross‑border enforcement, and AI accountability.&nbsp;</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><a href="https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2023/10/understanding-indias-new-data-protection-law?lang=en" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act</a></li>
</ul>



<p>Establishes a national framework for processing personal data, emphasising user consent, data minimisation and cross-border data transfer controls.&nbsp;</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Expanding regulations</strong></li>
</ul>



<p>Several states in America have enacted their own privacy laws (like California’s CCPA and <a href="https://matomo.org/blog/2023/09/vcdpa/">Virginia’s CDPA</a>), each setting unique requirements for data collection, user rights and business obligations. Use the <a href="https://iapp.org/resources/article/us-state-privacy-legislation-tracker/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">US State Privacy Legislation Tracker</a> to keep up with changes. </p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>AI accountability</strong></li>
</ul>



<p>The <a href="https://www.europarl.europa.eu/topics/en/article/20230601STO93804/eu-ai-act-first-regulation-on-artificial-intelligence" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">EU AI Act</a> outlines regulations for AI systems. It entered into force in 2024 and is being implemented in phases, with initial provisions beginning in 2025 and the majority becoming enforceable in August 2026. Full compliance across all categories extends into 2027. </p>



<p>Businesses should expect stricter disclosure requirements around:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Communicating with customers regarding AI.</li>



<li>Explaining how automated decisions are made.</li>



<li>Documenting the data sources used to train AI models.</li>
</ul>



<p>As a result of these tighter&nbsp;<a href="https://matomo.org/blog/2025/03/data-privacy-regulations-essential-knowledge-for-global-business/">data regulations</a>, we expect a continued increase in steep fines and public investigations into AI compliance. Regulators are already ramping up enforcement against major tech companies:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Meta’s <a href="https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/news/2023/12-billion-euro-fine-facebook-result-edpb-binding-decision_en" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">€1.2 billion fine</a> as a result of an EDPB binding decision, which found violations in data transfers between the EU and the U.S.</li>



<li><a href="https://www.cnil.fr/en/annual-report-2024" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">CNIL’s 2024 enforcement report</a> shows how France’s data protection authority introduced a simplified sanctioning process to resolve minor cases quickly. It allows the CNIL to issue fines without a full committee review. </li>
</ul>
